# The Game of Liberation: Field Blurbs 1–104

## 1. Vajra Hell

Vajra Hell is the terrifying result of abusing tantric practice and breaking its commitments. Wrathful visions and overwhelming fear imprison the mind, making it difficult to recognize the appearances as delusion.

## 2. Interminable Hell

Avīci is the deepest and hottest hell, where suffering continues without respite. The game associates it with actions that destroy the foundations of life and obstruct others’ access to the Dharma.

## 3. The Hot and Very Hot Hells

These neighboring hells subject beings to consuming heat, molten metal, and lakes of fire. They arise from destructive actions involving fire, deliberate deception, and grave harm to spiritual practitioners and their practice.

## 4. The Howling and Great Howling Hells

Intoxication that leads to harmful conduct, malicious lies, and abuse of trust ripen as the Howling and Great Howling Hells. The torment of the mouth and tongue reflects the destructive use of speech.

## 5. The Black Rope and Crushing Hells

The Black Rope Hell and Crushing Hell depict the consequences of theft and sexual misconduct compounded by violence. Beings are cut apart, crushed, or lured through forests of blades by the very desires that bind them.

## 6. Reviving Hell

Those whose actions have destroyed life repeatedly kill and are killed, only to revive and suffer again. This cycle continues until the karma sustaining it is exhausted.

## 7. The Cold Hells

Hatred and actions against the Dharma ripen as eight realms of devastating cold. Their torments progress from blistering and frozen speech to flesh splitting open like the petals of a lotus.

## 8. The Temporary Hells, or “Hell for a Day”

These isolated hells arise within earthly landscapes such as rivers, mountains, and deserts. Created by the actions of individuals or groups, they take innumerable forms of suffering.

## 9. Lord of the Dead (Yama)

Yama, the buffalo-headed Lord of the Dead, presides over the reckoning of deeds and the passage to another rebirth. His mirror, scales, and terrifying attendants embody the inescapable consequences of karma.

## 10. Hungry Ghosts (Preta)

Driven by greed, hungry ghosts experience insatiable hunger and thirst in a world where nourishment becomes inaccessible or revolting. Offerings dedicated to them provide relief from their distorted experience.

## 11. Animals

The animal realm portrays a life constrained by ignorance, vulnerability, and the struggle to survive. Beings endure predation, exploitation, and the search for food, with little freedom to pursue liberation.

## 12. Divine Animals

Miraculously born animals and other extraordinary beings inhabit the gardens, mountains, and retinues of the gods. Generosity may bring them beauty and power, while unresolved ignorance or aggression keeps them within saṃsāra.

## 13. World of the Nāgas

Nāgas inhabit waters and hidden palaces, guarding treasures and sometimes secret teachings. Generosity mixed with anger produces beings capable of great kindness, but also dangerous hostility.

## 14. Demon Island

Powerful, shape-shifting rākṣasas inhabit Demon Island, embodying the mixed consequences of generosity and pitiless killing. Through Padmasambhava’s intervention, their fierce energies can be bound to the service of the Dharma.

## 15. Asuras

The asuras possess power and splendor but are consumed by jealousy of the gods. Their endless struggle for the fruits of the wish-granting tree turns abundance into conflict.

## 16. Rudra: Black Freedom

Rudra embodies tantric accomplishment corrupted by self-importance and the desire to dominate. Without commitment to the welfare of others, apparent liberation becomes imprisonment in one’s own grasping.

## 17. Jambu Island — The Southern Continent

Human birth on Jambu Island combines suffering with the intelligence, opportunity, and freedom needed to practice the Dharma. Its preciousness lies in the possibility of meeting the Buddha’s teachings and seeking liberation.

## 18. The Western Continent — Enjoyment of Cattle (Aparagodānīya)

The Western Continent offers wealth and enjoyment amid lakes and pastures, beneath the red glow of Mount Meru. Although the Dharma is present, the inhabitants’ way of life gives it little opportunity to flourish.

## 19. The Eastern Continent — Noble Figure (Pūrvavideha)

East of Mount Meru lies a bright, tranquil continent whose inhabitants possess especially noble human forms. Its clear light reflects the mountain’s crystal face.

## 20. The Northern Continent — Kuru

The people of Kuru enjoy long lives, effortless abundance, and a peaceful communal existence. Their freedom from hardship also weakens the urgency to understand suffering and seek liberation.

## 21. Barbarism

This field represents human life in a society that normalizes cruelty, intolerance, and warfare. In the game’s moral geography, such conditions obstruct the encounter with the Dharma and perpetuate suffering.

## 22. Hinduism

The game portrays Brahmanical India as a flourishing world of learning, ethical discipline, and spiritual practice. From its Buddhist standpoint, attachment to an enduring self remains an obstacle to liberation.

## 23. Bön

Bön appears here as a tradition of ritual knowledge, divination, and communication with unseen beings. Within the game’s arrangement, it offers considerable scope for spiritual development outside the Buddhist paths.

## 24. The Heavenly Highway

The game begins in human life, where six colored roads open toward the six realms of rebirth. Karma is action: deeds of body, speech, and mind shape the journey ahead.

## 25. Entering the Vajrayāna

Entry into the Vajrayāna rests on a qualified teacher, bodhicitta, and faithfully maintained commitments. The common and special preliminaries prepare the practitioner for a swift and demanding path to Buddhahood.

## 26. Wheel-Turning King (Cakravartin)

The Cakravartin is a universal monarch whose generosity and ethical conduct ripen as just and compassionate rule. Accompanied by seven precious possessions, this ruler establishes others in wholesome action without killing.

## 27. Heaven of the Four Great Kings

The Four Great Kings guard the directions from the lower slopes of Mount Meru, overseeing the world and its many classes of spirits. Their protection extends to the gods above and to the practice of the Dharma.

## 28. Heaven of the Thirty-Three (Trāyastriṃśa)

Indra rules a magnificent heaven of gardens, jeweled palaces, and extraordinary pleasures at the summit of Mount Meru. Its inhabitants remain subject to conflict, impermanence, and rebirth when their merit is exhausted.

## 29. Heaven Without Fighting (Yāma)

The gods of Yāma dwell above worldly conflict in a heaven sustained by restraint and peaceful conduct. Even amid effortless enjoyment, they must guard against distraction and the eventual exhaustion of their good karma.

## 30. The Joyful (Tuṣita) Heaven

Tuṣita is the joyful heaven where Maitreya awaits his final human birth as a Buddha. Associated with learning and devotion to the Dharma, it offers a particularly favorable divine setting for spiritual aspiration.

## 31. Delighting in Emanations (Nirmāṇarati)

The gods of this heaven enjoy desirable appearances produced by their own minds. Generosity and refined conduct ripen as radiant bodies, magnificent surroundings, and pleasures summoned by a wish.

## 32. Ruling the Emanations of Others (Paranirmitavaśavartin)

These gods enjoy pleasures emanated for them by others, occupying the highest heaven of the Desire Realm. Their ruler, Māra, seeks to prevent practitioners from escaping his domain.

## 33. Vajrayāna: Lesser Stage of the Path of Accumulation

The game links this stage with Action Tantra, emphasizing purification, offerings, mantra, and devotion to the deity. Guided by empowerment and compassion, these practices gather merit and transform ordinary experience into pure appearance.

## 34. Mahākāla

Mahākāla is a wrathful protector whose fierce activity removes obstacles to the Dharma. His power expresses primal awareness and committed protection of the path.

## 35. The Form Realm (Rūpadhātu)

The Form Realm corresponds to increasingly refined meditative absorptions beyond attachment to sensory pleasures. Its radiant, subtle existence remains conditioned and does not by itself bring liberation.

## 36. The Formless Realm (Arūpadhātu)

The four formless attainments open onto infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception. Even these exceedingly subtle states remain within saṃsāra while attachment to existence persists.

## 37. Pure Abodes (Śuddhāvāsa)

The Pure Abodes are exalted levels of the Form Realm associated with the fourth meditative absorption and the five faculties. They are attained by āryas whose realization distinguishes them from ordinary beings.

## 38. Śrāvakayāna: Path of Accumulation (Saṃbhāramārga)

Disenchantment with saṃsāra leads the śrāvaka to gather merit and establish ethical discipline and meditation. Practices suited to the practitioner’s temperament weaken attachment, aversion, and confusion while preparing the mind for liberation.

## 39. Śrāvakayāna: Path of Application (Prayogamārga)

The śrāvaka applies meditative stability to understanding the Four Noble Truths and the conditioned nature of experience. Insight develops upon calm abiding, preparing the transition to direct realization.

## 40. Śrāvakayāna: Paths of Seeing and Meditation (Darśanamārga, Bhāvanāmārga)

On the Path of Seeing, the śrāvaka directly realizes the Four Noble Truths and overcomes fundamental mistaken views. The Path of Meditation removes the remaining defilements through sustained familiarity with that realization.

## 41. Vajrayāna: Intermediate Stage of the Path of Accumulation

Caryā Tantra combines outer ritual with inner meditation, approaching the deity as a companion. Mantra, visualization, attention to breath, and meditation on emptiness develop calm abiding and insight together.

## 42. Vajrayāna: Greater Stage of the Path of Accumulation

Yoga Tantra deepens identification with the awakened body, speech, mind, and activity of the Buddhas. The wisdom being enters the commitment being, and the practice of the seals establishes their inseparability.

## 43. Pratyekabuddhayāna: Path of Accumulation

The future pratyekabuddha continues gathering merit and practicing in solitude when no Buddha is present to teach. Earlier encounters with the Dharma bear fruit through perseverance across many lives.

## 44. Pratyekabuddhayāna: Path of Application

In solitude, the practitioner cultivates mindfulness of body, feelings, mind, and phenomena. Contemplating impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness prepares the way to awakening without a present teacher.

## 45. Pratyekabuddhayāna: Path of Seeing

Through examining birth and death, the solitary practitioner directly understands dependent origination. Seeing how suffering arises through conditions undermines belief in a continuing self and reveals the possibility of liberation.

## 46. Pratyekabuddhayāna: Path of Meditation

The pratyekabuddha deepens direct realization through sustained meditation, removing subtle attachment to existence. Extraordinary abilities may develop, but the central aim remains freedom from rebirth.

## 47. Pratyekabuddhayāna: Arhatship

The pratyekabuddha reaches arhatship and the Path of No More Learning, attaining liberation through solitary practice. Within the game’s Mahāyāna perspective, this achievement remains distinct from complete Buddhahood.

## 48. Cessation (Nirodha)

Cessation represents the arhat’s peace beyond the suffering of saṃsāra. In the game’s Mahāyāna account, Amitābha’s light rouses the practitioner toward the bodhisattva path and awakening for the benefit of all beings.

## 49. Vajrayāna: Path of Application — Warmth

The vase empowerment brings the practitioner into the maṇḍala and establishes the commitments of Unsurpassed Tantra. Purification and the introduction to bliss and emptiness plant the seed of nirmāṇakāya.

## 50. Vajrayāna: Path of Application — Summit

The secret empowerment introduces the disciple to the union of bliss and emptiness through the master’s ritual activity. It purifies and empowers the dimension of speech, planting the seed of saṃbhogakāya.

## 51. Śrāvakayāna: Arhatship

Following the Buddha’s instructions, the śrāvaka becomes an arhat, free from the defilements that sustain rebirth. Nirvāṇa is realized during life, while the remaining body and mind continue until death.

## 52. Mahāyāna: Lesser Stage of the Path of Accumulation

The Mahāyāna path begins with bodhicitta, the resolve to attain awakening for all beings. Generosity, ethical discipline, study, and meditation establish the accumulations and steady the mind.

## 53. Mahāyāna: Intermediate Stage of the Path of Accumulation

Bodhicitta becomes firmer as the practitioner strengthens faith, diligence, mindfulness, concentration, and discernment. The four right efforts cultivate wholesome qualities and gradually overcome the obstacles to meditation.

## 54. Mahāyāna: Greater Stage of the Path of Accumulation

On the greater Path of Accumulation, meditative stability supports the development of extraordinary abilities. These capacities help the bodhisattva receive teachings, deepen understanding, and become more effective in benefiting others.

## 55. Mahāyāna: Path of Application — Warmth (Ūṣman)

Intensive examination of the Dharma produces the first warmth of approaching realization. The practitioner investigates appearances as mental events while extending an impartial, caring attitude toward all beings.

## 56. Mahāyāna: Path of Application — Summit (Mūrdhan)

At the summit stage, the apparent independence of perceived objects is increasingly undermined. Understanding appearances as reflections of mind strengthens both meditation and the ability to guide others in wholesome conduct.

## 57. Vajrayāna: Path of Application — Acceptance

The third empowerment introduces discernment and primal awareness through the inseparability of bliss and emptiness. The experience of co-emergent bliss plants the seed for realizing dharmakāya.

## 58. Vajrayāna: Path of Application — Supreme Mundane Attribute

Through the word empowerment, the guru clarifies the meaning of the preceding experience of bliss, emptiness, and luminosity. This completes the preparatory introduction, whose meaning must still be fully realized through practice.

## 59. Shambhala

Shambhala is a hidden kingdom of tantric rulers and practitioners centered on the Kālacakra teachings. Its mountain refuge and lotus-shaped capital embody a realm of Dharma whose deepest access is also sought within the heart.

## 60. Potāla

Potāla is the sacred abode of Avalokiteśvara and Tārā, approached through difficult landscapes and purified perception. The ability to encounter its awakened inhabitants depends on the visitor’s own practice and receptivity.

## 61. Urgyan (Uḍḍiyāna)

Uḍḍiyāna is the wondrous land of the ḍākinīs, associated with tantric revelation and Padmasambhava’s lotus birth. Its stories present the transformation of ordinary passion and appearance into a path toward awakening.

## 62. Hindu Vidyādhara

The Hindu vidyādhara attains profound learning, meditative power, and mastery within the Brahmanical tradition. In the game’s Buddhist interpretation, liberation still requires release from attachment to an enduring self.

## 63. Mahāyāna: Path of Application — Acceptance (Kṣānti)

Acceptance brings a stable openness to emptiness and the Four Noble Truths, supported by an unwavering commitment to others. Grasping at the perceiving subject weakens as the five faculties become unshakable strengths.

## 64. Mahāyāna: Path of Application — Supreme Mundane Attribute (Laukikāgradharma)

The practitioner reaches the highest stage of mundane preparation, immediately preceding direct realization. Discernment and skillful means are ready to unfold into the bodhisattva’s increasingly effective activity for others.

## 65. Vidyādhara of the Bön Tradition

The Bön vidyādhara unites ritual mastery, discernment, and the practice of the consort to realize primordial knowing. The game places this extraordinary accomplishment at the summit of its non-Buddhist paths.

## 66. First Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The water empowerment begins the game’s ten Vajrayāna bhūmis, purifying bodily activity and preparing the practitioner to serve as a vajra master. The capacity to encounter Buddhas, manifest emanations, and benefit beings begins to unfold.

## 67. Vidyādhara Among the Gods of the Desire Realm

A vidyādhara among the gods of the Desire Realm possesses youthful radiance and extraordinary worldly siddhis. Mastery of mantra brings powers such as flight, invisibility, and the fulfillment of wishes.

## 68. Vidyādhara of the Form Realm

These vidyādharas dwell as radiant skyfarers beyond ordinary sensory enjoyment. Through mantra and yoga without images, they cultivate subtle meditative stability, insight, and exceptionally long lives.

## 69. Vajrayāna Wheel-Turning King

The sovereign of the vidyādharas unites vast learning with immediate realization and exceptional accomplishment. Teaching through both words and personal example, this ruler guides beings according to their capacities.

## 70. Realm of Accomplished Activity

Amoghasiddhi’s northern pure land expresses the complete effectiveness of awakened activity. Jealousy is transformed into precise, unobstructed action that removes hindrances and fulfills the aims of the Dharma.

## 71. First Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the first bhūmi, joy arises from directly seeing selflessness and the nearness of awakening. Generosity takes precedence as the bodhisattva renews vast aspirations and begins the noble path of benefiting beings.

## 72. Vidyādhara of the Eight Siddhis

Through sādhana, the vidyādhara masters eight worldly siddhis, including extraordinary sight, swiftness, transformation, and passage through barriers. These accomplishments remain distinct from Buddhahood, which requires realization of emptiness.

## 73. Second Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The crown empowerment purifies bodily obstacles and plants the seeds of the Buddha’s physical marks. Freedom from concern for worldly honor is joined with the compassion needed to guide others.

## 74. Third Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The vajra empowerment introduces the nonconceptual realization associated with awakened mind. Grasping at an enduring self is relinquished as the practitioner’s understanding becomes firmly established.

## 75. Fourth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The bell empowerment develops the capacity for awakened speech. The practitioner becomes able to communicate the Dharma in ways suited to the many dispositions and circumstances of beings.

## 76. Realm of Jeweled Peaks (Ratnakūṭa)

Ratnasambhava’s southern pure land shines with the abundance of the wish-granting jewel. The primal awareness of equality transforms pride and possessiveness into generosity and the fulfillment of others’ needs.

## 77. Land of Bliss (Sukhāvatī)

In Amitābha’s Sukhāvatī, beings are born among lotus ponds and jeweled palaces, with ready access to the Dharma. Created through vast aspirations, this pure land supports progress toward awakening and expresses the transformation of attachment into compassion.

## 78. Fourth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the fourth bhūmi, diligence joined with discernment burns through emotional and cognitive obscurations. The bodhisattva intensifies practice while remaining humble, free from sectarian attachment, and grounded in the middle way.

## 79. Third Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

The third bhūmi perfects patience through an unwavering willingness to endure hardship for the Dharma and remain available to others. Insight and meditative abilities illuminate a life devoted to teaching and compassionate service.

## 80. Second Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

The second bhūmi is immaculate through the perfection of ethical discipline. Abandoning the ten unwholesome actions, the bodhisattva combines discernment with conduct that protects and nourishes life.

## 81. Fifth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The empowerment of permission completes the path of ripening in the game’s sequence. Receiving the vajra, bell, and empowerment name, the practitioner is authorized to teach and confer empowerments in accordance with the commitments.

## 82. Sixth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

Intensive deity practice begins to bring the four empowerments to fulfillment. As the subtle winds enter and dissolve in the central channel, meditation at the heart opens the way to deeper realization.

## 83. Seventh Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The practitioner experiences a semblance of luminosity through the union of bliss and emptiness. Even radiant, nonconceptual absorption must deepen into realization through continued practice and discernment.

## 84. Supreme Heaven (Akaniṣṭha)

In Akaniṣṭha’s Vajradhātu Palace, bodhisattvas of the tenth bhūmi receive the Buddhas’ highest instructions. This supreme pure realm is the setting for the teaching activity of saṃbhogakāya.

## 85. Realm of Superjoy (Abhirati)

Akṣobhya’s eastern pure land embodies unshakable presence amid radiant lakes, jewel trees, and lotus blossoms. Hatred is transformed into mirror-like primal awareness, clear and undisturbed by what appears.

## 86. Seventh Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the seventh bhūmi, skillful means expands into vast and spontaneous activity for the welfare of beings. The bodhisattva moves freely through diverse teachings and manifestations without abandoning either realization or compassion.

## 87. Sixth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

The sixth bhūmi brings discernment face to face with dependent origination and the emptiness of phenomena. This realization perfects the other pāramitās while freeing the bodhisattva from attachment to either saṃsāra or a private peace.

## 88. Fifth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the fifth bhūmi, deep meditation is joined with the demanding work of guiding others. The bodhisattva applies learning, arts, sciences, and the teachings on truth without losing sight of the empty nature of experience.

## 89. Eighth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

An illusory body arises from the subtle winds and the understanding that appearances are mind-made. It remains impure until united with luminosity that directly realizes emptiness.

## 90. Ninth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The illusory body is purified through meditative absorption into luminosity. Appearances are experienced as empty, luminous, and inseparable from mind, allowing extraordinary capacities to unfold.

## 91. Tenth Vajrayāna Bhūmi

The illusory body and luminous mind unite at the stage of learning, approaching the full expression of saṃbhogakāya. Compassion sustains activity for others as the practitioner approaches the union beyond learning of Buddhahood.

## 92. Great Saṃbhogakāya

Saṃbhogakāya displays the perfected qualities of Buddhahood and teaches advanced bodhisattvas in pure realms. Like the sun radiating light, it manifests nirmāṇakāya emanations suited to beings throughout the world.

## 93. Great Dharmakāya

Dharmakāya is revealed as obscurations clear, beyond conceptual extremes and the division of self and other. Its primal awareness knows both the empty nature of phenomena and the full range of their appearances.

## 94. Tenth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the tenth bhūmi, the bodhisattva becomes a cloud of Dharma, showering teachings that relieve suffering. Primal awareness, countless samādhis, and vast emanations prepare the culmination in Buddhahood.

## 95. Ninth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

The ninth bhūmi brings unimpeded knowledge of Dharma, meaning, language, and eloquence. The bodhisattva teaches with extraordinary precision, addressing many capacities and questions without obstruction.

## 96. Eighth Sūtrayāna Bhūmi

On the eighth bhūmi, realization becomes unshakable and beneficial activity unfolds without deliberate striving. Reminded of the original vows by the Buddhas, the bodhisattva continues toward complete awakening for all beings.

## 97. Adopting a Physical Form

Through nirmāṇakāya, the Buddha appears within the world to establish the Dharma and benefit beings. The life of Śākyamuni begins with descent from Tuṣita, human birth, and the unfolding of the deeds of awakening.

## 98. Going Forth (Pravrajyā)

Confronted with aging, sickness, death, and the example of a renunciant, the bodhisattva recognizes the limits of palace life. Leaving behind royal comforts, he sets out to discover freedom from saṃsāra.

## 99. Ascetic Practices (Tapas)

The bodhisattva explores the teachings and severe ascetic practices of his time, finding that neither exalted absorption nor self-mortification brings final liberation. Accepting nourishment, he turns toward the Bodhi tree and the path to awakening.

## 100. Conquest of Māra

Māra confronts the bodhisattva with fear, violence, doubt, and desire beneath the Bodhi tree. Unmoved, the bodhisattva calls the earth to witness his accumulated merit and overcomes the forces obstructing awakening.

## 101. Buddhahood

Through the night, the bodhisattva realizes the course of rebirth, the causes of suffering, and their cessation. With the complete awakening of primal awareness, he becomes a Buddha, free from the ignorance that sustains saṃsāra.

## 102. Turning the Wheel of Dharma

Moved by compassion, the Buddha teaches the path of liberation, beginning with the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path. The turnings of the Dharma wheel unfold teachings suited to different capacities, including selflessness and the emptiness of phenomena.

## 103. Demonstration of Miracles

The Buddha uses miracles to reach those whom words alone do not persuade. Transforming danger and displaying extraordinary mastery, he inspires confidence and guides beings toward the Dharma.

## 104. Nirvāṇa

The Buddha’s nirmāṇakāya demonstrates passing into nirvāṇa after completing its teaching activity. His final instructions emphasize impermanence and diligent practice, directing disciples to bring the path to fulfillment.
